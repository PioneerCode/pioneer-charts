When To Use An Area Chart

An area chart is a line chart with the space beneath each line filled in. The fill turns the line into an amount: the reader sees not just where a series is but how much of it there is, so an area chart suits values that are volumes or totals over time — requests served, energy used, revenue earned, people in a building. Reach for one when:

  • the size of each series matters as much as its trend, and its values start from a meaningful zero;
  • there are only a few series, two or three, so the overlapping fills stay readable;
  • one series is mostly larger than the others, or the series rarely cross, so each fill keeps its own band.

Use the line chart instead when the series cross often, when there are more than three or four of them, or when the y axis doesn't start at zero — there, the fills would stack up into a muddy block, or claim an amount that isn't there. To compare totals at a single moment rather than over time, a bar chart is easier to read; and for how a single total splits into parts, see the pie and donut charts.

How The Areas Are Drawn

Each series in data becomes one filled shape, running from its points down to the bottom of the plot — the y axis' domainMin, 0 by default. The series are overlaid rather than stacked: every area starts from that same floor, at half opacity, in the order they appear in data, so a later series is painted over an earlier one and the overlap shows as a blend of the two colors. The area itself has no outline; its points carry the dots, the tooltips and (dotClicked), exactly as on the line chart.

  • Order matters. Put the series that is usually largest first, so the smaller ones are drawn over it rather than hidden behind it.
  • Mind the floor. Because the fill runs down to yAxis.domainMin, raising it to zoom in on a narrow band crops every area at the same line. That's fine for a line chart; on an area chart it exaggerates the differences between series, so keep the floor at zero when the size of the area is the point.
  • Gaps stay gaps. A point with no numeric value breaks the area there instead of dropping it to zero, so missing data isn't drawn as a dip.
  • Hidden series keep their color. A series with hide: true isn't drawn, but keeps its place in the palette, so a legend that toggles series never reshuffles the colors of the rest.

Stacked Areas

The chart doesn't stack series for you, but a stacked area chart — each series sitting on top of the ones before it, so the top edge is the total — is a few lines of data preparation away: give each series the running total up to and including it, then reverse the order so the tallest band (the total) is drawn first and every smaller one over it.

Two things change with stacked data: a point's tooltip and the hover crosshair show the running total rather than the series' own value (a custom tooltip can show the original, from a field you keep on the point), and the series are now in reverse order, so pass a reversed colorOverride, or list a legend's items in the same reversed order, if the colors need to match something else.
