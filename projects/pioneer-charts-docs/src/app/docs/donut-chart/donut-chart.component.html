When To Use A Donut Chart

A donut chart shows how a whole splits into parts, like a pie chart, and adds one thing: a place for the number the parts add up to. Use it when that number is what readers should see first and the split is the detail — tickets by status around the count of open tickets, a budget by department around the total spend, a storage quota around the space left. It's also the natural shape for a single value out of a whole, like a completion rate, drawn as a ring.

The usual pie chart advice holds: keep it to about five slices or fewer, and switch to a bar chart when the parts are close in size or there are many of them. And if there's nothing worth putting in the middle, the pie chart gives the same slices more room.

One Value As A Ring

Two slices — the part and the rest — with a thin ring and the percentage in the center make a progress or completion ring. Give the remainder a quiet color with colorOverride so the part stands out; the remainder is still a slice, with its own tooltip and click.

The Center Label

The hole holds up to two lines: label, large, and subLabel, smaller, under it. Each is sized from the hole — the label up to 40px, the sub label up to 16px — and shrunk until it fits across it, so the same config reads well on a small dashboard tile and a full-width page. A line that would have to shrink below 8px to fit is left out rather than drawn illegibly, so keep them short: a number for the label, a word or two for the sub label.

The chart doesn't add up the slices for you, which leaves the label free to be anything — a total, a percentage, a currency amount, a status. To show the total, sum the values when you build the config and format it the way the rest of your page does:

Ring Thickness

innerRadius sets the size of the hole as a fraction of the chart's radius, and with it how thick the ring is. The default, 0.6, balances the two: room for a large label, and slices thick enough to compare. Around 0.75 to 0.85 makes a thin ring that suits a single value or a row of small dashboard charts, where the label is the point. Below about 0.4 the hole gets too small for a label to fit at a readable size, and at 0 there's no hole at all — the chart draws a plain pie.
